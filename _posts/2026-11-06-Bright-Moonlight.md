Li Bai (or Li Po, among other names) is the most famous Chinese poet. His most famous poem translates too *Quiet Night Thought*. This is my favorite translation

-------------------------------


床前明月光

疑是地上霜

舉頭望明月

低頭思故鄉

Chúang Quían Míng Yúe Guáng

Yí Shì Dì Shàng Shuang

JǔTóu Wàng Míng Yúe

Dī Tóu Sī Gù Xīang

-------------------------------

Before my bed

There is bright moonlight

                  So that it seems

Like frost on the ground:

                  lifting my head

I watch the bright moon,

                  Lowering my head

I dream that I am home.


This poem was written in the Tang dynasty (around 726 AD), likely when Li Bai was in Chang‘an (now Xi’an), far from his home in Sichuan. Li Bai was a drunkard and a wanderer. He was a poet obsessed with the moon, which appears in many of his poems. His writing process was heavily informed by heavy drinking; he would get drunk with friends, then write. Maybe you can relate to this process. As a lover of the moon and drink, he died in perhaps the most poetic way possible. Here is the story of Li Bai’s death, as I imagine it, so heavily embellished.

 One night, the great poet Li Bai, now old and whitehaired, went out to drink with some friends. Many, though not all, of these friends were old and whitehaired, and together they set up to drink the evening away. Eventually, the evening passed into darkness. Li Bai saw a nearby river and declared is intention to go sailing. His friends asked why. Li Bai was too drunk to respond, and they were too drunk to stop him. He boarded the boat and sailed onto the lake. In this time, the moon rose above the lake and could be seen as a reflection on the water. Mistaking the reflection for the actual object, the great poet leapt into the face of his celestial lover. He drowned. Drunk and under the moon.

Li Bai’s poems speak to me not only through centuries, but through vastly different worlds, and translation too. He has made me realize I don’t spend enough time looking at the moon, nor the stars.

Bright Moonlight is one of my favorite poems ever. He talks about frost. I will go a year without cold, which is something I am happy about. I prefer the warmth. Yet. Even though the chill I associate with winter and frost goes beyond physical feeling and into the emotional realm. And even though I have no desire to go back to the school that I despise. I find myself dreaming of frost. Home is bound to the bones, it seems, and that can hurt.

In this poem, there are three phases to action. The first: raising the head, the second: lowering the head, the third: dreaming. To me, in these three actions Li Bai captures an entire life in a single scene. It’s a way of saying from dust to dust; from pillow to pillow. And dreaming – recall: the cousin of death – is in-between. His poems are wonderful because in each they first set some landscape, with or without action (similarly to man Elizabeth Bishop poems). Then, via some device or another, force the reader into a realization. In this one, for me, the realization that the distance home is the same distance of a lifetime.
