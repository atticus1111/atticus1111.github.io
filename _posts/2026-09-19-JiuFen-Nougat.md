Beauty

They say that Jiufen inspired Spirited away. They are full of shit. Jiufen is a beautiful old town that has been kneaded into a mountain side in the north of Taiwan. At night, the red lamps turn on, and all the cramped alley ways are filled with autumn light. Which is where the false Spirited Away inspiration comes from, a falsehood borne on a torrent of travelers and tourist promotional. The buildings are old and wooden; the stores are modern and touristy. I saw at least 5 shops selling the number 1 nougat cookies, I got some and it was good, but number 1 is a stretch. Climbing to the top of the town and looking down across the rooftops I couldn’t make out the packed streets that I had walked through. Each street is only a couple meters wide, and the orange light is blocked by the buildings. There were a lot of tourists, so I was forced to walk at a snail’s pace at times. There are a lot of tea shops, tea seems to be the aesthetic thing to do here, another box to check (thank Instagram maybe). Jiufen overlooks a small harbor and a couple islands. The best part was looking out across this coastline. Taiwan’s volcanic terrain makes the coasts look like moss covered bubble wrap. The mountains rise out of the water and playfully roll into one another, in the shape of a ball thrown skywards. Sometimes they will rise steeply enough that the dense forest gives way to dark rocks. Everything fades into the grey background of low hanging clouds. Some things are too beautiful to picture. When I snap a photo, I feel that I am marring the image.

I am reminded of Chinese landscapes, which are incredibly beautiful. I have seen a some of the same landscape paintings a couple times at the National Palace Museum. I like Chinese landscapes a lot more than European ones. Mostly because the Chinese landscapes (in my completely untrained opinion) use negative space and color far better. Mountains rise out of the page, which could be clouds or the sea, sometimes both. Vivid blues and greens mountaintops stand out against grey stone and yellowing paper. Little people in temples are hiding amid the trees. Some of the ones with lots of people make give me the same feeling as where’s waldo books. The feeling of getting lost in worlds long ago and far away. The landscapes are often as long as the entire exhibition room. The details suck me in, and the negative space keeps me curious. Meticulous detail is combined with nothingness, which is tantalizing to look at. I often want just a little more than there is. The restraint that these landscapes use differ from European ones. Which are also stunning with vivid colors and serene visages, but pack an entire country into one paining. There is no room to breathe. Some European landscape artists would combine scenes from several vastly different places, making a mashup painting. These paintings are kinda like a fast-food-combo-meal, I guess. Delicious and full of color and light, but overly satisfying. And Chinese landscapes, are sparce and stunning, with just enough to keep you wanting more.

I went to the National Palace Museum before going to Jiufen. I describe my favorite of the paintings in the palace museum with the same adjectives as the area around Jiufen. Surrounded by such wonderful landscape, it seems no wonder that the painters rendered such beautiful works. But I think a lot of it is about noticing the ordinary. Being here has made me realize how extraordinary the mountains are back home. I was describing sunset over the flatirons in the fall to a friend earlier, because a sunset over these mountains begets comparison to a sunset back home. There is a majesty to our mountains and plains that isn’t here. Here, the mountains are covered in mist, atmosphere, and clouds with rock faces obscured by trees and greenery. Our mountains stand tall and proud, visible from everywhere. Maybe what makes great artists great, and great adventure great, is the way that we notice the ordinary once again. Lately, I am reminded of Elizibeth Bishops great poem *Questions of Travel*:

There are too many waterfalls here; the crowded streams

hurry too rapidly down to the sea,

and the pressure of so many clouds on the mountaintops

makes them spill over the sides in soft slow-motion,

turning to waterfalls under our very eyes.

—For if those streaks, those mile-long, shiny, tearstains,

aren't waterfalls yet,

in a quick age or so, as ages go here,

they probably will be.

But if the streams and clouds keep travelling, travelling,

the mountains look like the hulls of capsized ships,

slime-hung and barnacled.

Think of the long trip home.

Should we have stayed at home and thought of here?

Where should we be today?

Is it right to be watching strangers in a play

in this strangest of theatres?

What childishness is it that while there's a breath of life

in our bodies, we are determined to rush

to see the sun the other way around?

The tiniest green hummingbird in the world?

To stare at some inexplicable old stonework,

inexplicable and impenetrable,

at any view,

instantly seen and always, always delightful?

Oh, must we dream our dreams

and have them, too?

And have we room

for one more folded sunset, still quite warm?

But surely it would have been a pity

not to have seen the trees along this road,

really exaggerated in their beauty,

not to have seen them gesturing

like noble pantomimists, robed in pink.

—Not to have had to stop for gas and heard

the sad, two-noted, wooden tune

of disparate wooden clogs

carelessly clacking over

a grease-stained filling-station floor.

(In another country the clogs would all be tested.

Each pair there would have identical pitch.)

—A pity not to have heard

the other, less primitive music of the fat brown bird

who sings above the broken gasoline pump

in a bamboo church of Jesuit baroque:

three towers, five silver crosses.

—Yes, a pity not to have pondered,

blurr'dly and inconclusively,

on what connection can exist for centuries

between the crudest wooden footwear

and, careful and finicky,

the whittled fantasies of wooden footwear

and, careful and finicky,

the whittled fantasies of wooden cages.

—Never to have studied history in

the weak calligraphy of songbirds' cages.

—And never to have had to listen to rain

so much like politicians' speeches:

two hours of unrelenting oratory

and then a sudden golden silence

in which the traveler takes a notebook, writes:

"Is it lack of imagination that makes us come

to imagined places, not just stay at home?

Or could Pascal have been not entirely right

about just sitting quietly in one's room?

Continent, city, country, society:

the choice is never wide and never free.

And here, or there . . . No. Should we have stayed at home,

wherever that may be?"
