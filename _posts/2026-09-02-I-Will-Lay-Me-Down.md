It rains almost every day here. Low clouds crowd the skyline; they compete with skyscrapers in majesty. People walk under awnings and pop them to cross the street. Herds of umbrellas crowd the doorways of stores, drying off while their owners’ shop. This city has chosen umbrellas over raincoats; coats can get hot. Only the motorcyclists wear plastic coats; ones that hang to the knees. Walkers waltz past each other’s umbrellas trying not to hit each other. Eyes on the ground, feet following the same dry (ish) path. Under trees, the water falls in heavier, bigger drops. You can hear the changes on your umbrella. Pitter-patter of rain and the passing cars give beat and rhythm to it all. Taipei is dancing in the rain.

There is a typhoon coming, and it rains every day. My feet, somedays, are damp all day. I avoid umbrellas because I forget them everywhere, but I like to stay dry too, so I have managed. I got caught out in the rain for a whole day, and what I expected to be a nice day of wandering turned into a scene from blade runner. I was feeling a little put out from that and the next day I decided to spend my time inside museums. I began with the National Taiwan University (NTU) museums. But they are closed on Tuesdays. Undeterred, I made my way to my next stop, the drinking water museum. One of my favorite things to do in any city I go to is to learn about the history of their drinking water. Water is the most important human resource, and its reveals a lot about a place.


The drinking water museum is housed in this stately, European looking building. Surprise surprise, it was built during the Japanese occupation of Taiwan, during Japan’s Meiji restoration. Taiwan didn’t have a centralized water treatment plant until Japanese rule. This plant was the first in Taiwan. The building was designed by a Japanese architect Moriyama Matsunosuke while the actual treatment was designed by a Scottish engineer Williiam K Burton. Burton died of malaria before the plant was finished in 1907, so never got to see its completion. Housing about 7 water pumps, the facility would pump water from the nearby Hsintien Creek, treat it, and pump it to a nearby storage facility. Unfortunately, there wasn’t a complete treatment train provided L, but from what I saw the facility utilized a slow sand filter to do its treatment. I suspect there wasn’t any coagulation/flocculation/sedimentation because I didn’t see any of those basins. And I am unsure if chlorination was used. By the time of the facilities construction, chlorination had gained acceptance and was used to great effect for reducing waterborne diseases. So, it seems probable that chlorination was used to treat the water. The facility used a slow sand filter, which has a low Total Organic Carbon (TOC) removal precent, and a large storage basin. And considering that Taipei is a subtropical region, I’d expect there is a higher amount of TOC in the surface water that the plant treats, and so a high amount of TOC in the treated water. Combining a high TOC, with chlorine can get dangerous because Disinfection By-Products (DBPs) can form. DPBs are toxic and pose significant health risks. DPB formation is not instantaneous, and it takes time, so the chlorine and TOC residing in the storage basin for a significant amount of time may have increased the DBP concentration beyond healthy levels. For reference current DBP standards are less than 0.06 mg/L. Balancing removal of dangerous viruses and microorganisms in water via chlorine with formation of DBPs is one of the key challenges of drinking water treatment. This section is not important to Taiwan, but I think its cool and good to think about our water, so it is included.

In any case, this was the first industrial scale facility on Taiwan, which is significant. Japanese colonization brought a lot of prosperity to the island, like in water, or in the development of Taiwan’s rail system. These advancements were mostly to speed up the exploitation of the island. Better water can support more workers (and their overseers), and an efficient rail speeds up the transportation of crops from their source to the port. Colonization’s legacy is very complicated. On one hand it means less people die from waterborne disease, and on the other there are cultural shifts, dehumanization, and exploitation. This practice hasn’t ended. Global development saves people from waterborne diseases, while at the same time often (though not always!) exporting ‘savior complexes,’ capitalistic systems, and cultural destruction. What even constitutes development? For example, ancient Egyptians practiced an early form of coagulation by using alum to increase precipitate of solids in water. Are they any more or less developed than the ancient Greeks who lacked coagulation technology?

*Taiwan Travelogue* tells the story of a Japanese travel writer who comes to Taiwan during its Japanese occupation to write about the island. The central conflict arises between the main character and her guide, a Taiwanese local. During her travels, the writer, Chizuko, attributes Taiwan’s success to the Japanese imperial regime. Chizuko laments cultural destruction but ultimately thinks that development of the island is a net positive. The hand of progress is brutal, but its work is beautiful. Implicitly, she tries to conform her guide to Japanese standards, assuming that her way of life can ‘save’ her guide from her life. For example, Chizuko buys her guide Aizō a Japanese kimono, so that Aizō can fit into to Japanese culture better. Colonization is a nation’s expansion, and imperialism is the maintenance of an empire. The core of imperialism is extraction. Imperialism takes from new lands for the purpose of profit, destroying the new lands in a careless process. Taiwan travelogue shows the subtle machinery of imperialism at work through Chizuko’s thoughts on Taiwan and how she treats Aizō. Imperialist consumption is carless, it consumes without thought. Consumptive practices see countries as commodities, as things to ‘do.’ Hearing foreigners here talk about ‘doing’ countries reminds me of this consumption. How can one ‘do’ a country or a culture? Engage in thoughtful consumption. If Arendt’s point in *The Human Condition* is to get us too ‘think what we are doing’ in general my point is, we ought to ‘think what we are doing’ for travel.

Mindless consumption invades every aspect of our lives. We produce inordinate amounts of waste from packaging, clothes, and water. Water waste is especially insidious because of how important water is. It is probably the most important resource for our species and our planet. But people leave the tap running constantly, flush too much, or consume water intense food. We need to use water and it’s not bad that we use tap water, that we flush the toilet, or eat food. It’s how we do it where the issues arrive. The issue is how we consume without thinking about what it is we consume.
