
My flight arrived at 4:30 in the morning. I showed up to my hostel hours before my check in exhausted. After a short nap I got to my feet and started walking. Taipei is a huge, dense city full of miles of 4-6 story apartment blocks with commercial first floors built out of reinforced concrete. I am used to American cities dominated by bricks or glass. Denver’s old downtown is all red brick; the red brick buildings were a response to a [fire in Denver](https://www.cpr.org/2019/07/01/denvers-brick-legacy-is-rooted-in-a-fire-and-the-smell-of-burning-bacon-in-the-air/) that destroyed most of the city. Chicago is distinctive for its downtown tile, spurred by the ceramic making industry, and Greystones, a consequence of abundant limestone and again, fire. Materials that make up a city, are tell the city’s history. In Taipei, after the Peoples Republic of China (the communists) won the Chinese civil war, the Republic of China (ROC) lead by the Kuomintang (KMT) was expelled to Taiwan. Taiwan suddenly had millions of immigrants from the mainland. The KMT government of Taiwan relied heavily on USAID money, and US contractors to build up much of the Taipei. To quickly build housing, contractors relied on new-ish, cheap, quick reinforced concrete for the buildings. One block, Minsheng, was built by a US firm and the style of it, 4-6 story block housing of cheap concrete, came to define the city. These buildings ate up the older Japanese buildings and remnants of Qin dynasty construction in the city. Now, these cheap, relatively short buildings are destroyed, and space is made way for modern skyscrapers. Buildings with elevators that extend the maximum height of a building significantly. Beyond material, Taipei’s layout is interesting. Large spacious avenues planned by the Japanese carve massive blocks out of the city, and smaller alleyways cut through the massive blocks, breaking them up. It is kind of like if Chicago had blocks 5 times bigger, with many more alleys. Despite the turnover remnants of Japanese occupation are still present in the city. The Japanese constructed much still used infrastructure, like the president’s palace. Stylistically, these buildings are a confusing. The buildings look like a Japanese take on western architecture, which is exactly what they are. During Japan’s Meiji restoration – when these were built -- Japan sent many of its students to study abroad in western universities.

Now, I didn’t know all this the moment that I stepped out of my hostel. I read about the city later and have written it here. But the city’s history is written into every stone and every concrete pour that makes up the built environment. History gives us a glimpse into the cultural understanding of a place. My first couple days in Taipei I have mostly spent wandering, trying to gain an understanding of an entirely new culture. One of the most impactful ways to understand a culture is through its food, which I what I set out for as I left my hostel.

I came to Taipei main station, and I started following signs for Zhongshan metro mall. I felt like I was walking for miles underground following signs to the metro mall and still hadn’t passed it. As I walked, I smelled something delicious, butter and bread, mmmhmmm. The smell came from a self-serve bakery place, like Tous Les Jous in Denver for the initiated. Far hungrier than I realized, I got a potato cheese bread and an egg tart. The potato bread was tasty, albeit a little chewier than I was hoping. The egg tart was very eggy, and the crust was nice and flaky, so in taste unremarkable. Egg tarts likely migrated from Macau – a Portuguese colony.  The Portuguese ‘discovered’ Taiwan in the 16^th^ century and named it Ilha Formosa, meaning [beautiful island in Portuguese](https://islandfolklore.com/taiwan-names-formosa/). However, the Portuguese did not establish a permanent colony on Taiwan, their concern was the Chinese mainland, so Macau became their east Asian holding. Dutch people came by the island in the 17^th^ century, also searching for an east Asian foothold. They were told to go to Taiwan instead and settled the area near modern day Tainan. Siraya peoples told the Dutch colonizers the name of the area was called “Tayoan”, which the Dutch mutated into “Tayovan.”

After finishing my food, I still had a few hours to kill before I could check in to my hostel. I wandered over to Huàshān creative park, looking for nothing in particular. Huàshān used to be a distillery, and the distillation buildings have been converted into a bustling commercial center. Inside I got some mango milk ice which was tasty, but mostly because the mango was delicious. Ice is too cold for my teeth unfortunately. Mango is delicious and all fruit is better here. It is incomparable to Colorado’s fruit unfortunately. As I was sitting and eating my mango ice, some old guy came and sat with me. I think he was making fun of me, he talked fast Mandarin and I didn’t understand, which I let him know (in Mandarin). Still, I was able to pick some things up, like his remark that Americans are so tall compared to Taiwanese. Which is true, I am typically the tallest here. After I finished my mango ice and bade the old guy farewell, I returned to the hostel and checked in. I took a twenty-minute nap that turned into five hours. I woke up well rested and hungry.

I went out to Níngxìa night market for food. I walked through the stalls to see my options and decided on a scallion pancake. It was amazing; frozen trader joes are nothing in comparison. These were thin and crispy, so each bite was very satisfying. There was cooked egg on top of the pancake which gave it a fuller flavor. On top of the egg the vendor spread some spice and a couple seasonings. It was salty with strong umami flavors, it could have been a bit spicier, but having not too much spice was nice because it did not overtake the flavors of the pancake. A top-notch meal. Afterwards I bought some peanut ice cream. Similarly to earlier, the ice cream was too cold, but the peanut was tasty and there was cilantro inside as well, which was an interesting, tasty flavor. I returned home and had a beer and played cards with other members of the hostel until late. My sleep cycle is very off right now; it is hard to regulate when I feel tired.

Next day I went to go on a tour of old town Taiwan. I missed the tour by 30 minutes; I thought it was at 10 when it was at 9:30. Whoops. Undeterred, I went to the nearby Cheng Kai Shek memorial. The memorial itself is in a beautiful park flanked by the national theater and concert hall with the CKS memorial as the center point of the park. In some ways I was reminded of the Lincon memorial. Which got me thinking if there is any other more important American monument. Maybe the Statue of Liberty, but the French built it, so it doesn’t count. The CKS monument is interesting because it is a monument to an authoritarian man, it is a monument to authoritarianism. Again, I thought back to the states. Confederate monuments are either revered or torn down, they aren’t altered or included in more complex discussions about our history.

The building has three levels to it; the base, the hall, and the roof. There are many symbolic components to the building, many of which I do not pick up on. Scale wise, the monument is far larger than the Lincon Memorial. After visiting the massive statue of CKS I went to the first floor to see the exhibit on display there. The basement exhibit is all about Taiwan’s struggle for freedom against autocracy. A timeline of modern uprisings (they erroneously call many of them revolutions when they are really rebellions, but whatever) against authoritarian regimes. Its centerpiece is a history of Taiwan’s road to democracy and freedom of speech. In Taiwan, after the second world war, the Chinese civil war broke out. The Peoples Republic of China, led by chairman Mao, won the war and forced the Republic of China to the island of Taiwan. Chaing Kai Shek was the president of the ROC. With the pretext of combating communist influence, martial law was installed across the island. An era of violent oppression and suppression of ideas called the white terror followed. White terror was eventually overthrown by democratic protests, where the wild lily movement in 1990 was a keystone to the democratic transition. It ends with a call to fight for freedom; democracy is an ephemeral fruit that requires strong institutions to uphold. Democracy itself is not self-sustaining, and this exhibit shows how we fight for its continuation. I understand the desire to pull down monuments to criminal leaders. But I think that these monuments are important to preserve because they tell us that we need to fight for our ideals. The exhibit ends with a Milian Kundera quote, “The struggle of man against power is the struggle of memory against forgetting.” We cannot forget our history, and we shouldn’t forget how we have glorified racists and killers.

Back to food. After the monument I went to get some more scallion pancakes. There was such a line! Like many places in Taiwan, this was hardly more than a food stand cut into the side of a building. I was watching the chef make up the pancakes and wondered what it is like to live as such a chef. Spending all day in the humidity and the heat while also standing over a hot flame, moving constantly. I can imagine it somewhat, having worked at an intense, high heat food stand in a bar at [Samosa Shop](https://www.instagram.com/samosashopco/?hl=en). There is a lot to be said about food made quickly in tiny kitchens. So much effort goes into every part of the meal. Working on these meals and recipes is interesting because it is about transforming only a few ingredients into a whole menu. At Samosa Shop, I was always impressed by my Boss’ ability to do this. Our menu was diverse and interesting, and all the separation between mushroom sandwich and mushroom nuggets was all in the preparation and cooking. There is so much effort that goes into making food that is quick make and eat before it even arrives at the stall. At this scallion pancake stall, the menu was short, no [cheesecake factory](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DcRyWmQtQxy/), just variations on scallion pancakes. Pankake with cheese, egg, ham, and basil in every permutation. Thrilling. Little bags of premade scallion pancakes were stored by the side of the grill. Hundreds of little bags that looked like the bag that Darla gets Merlin in, but instead of clownfish, swirled dough with scallions. Scallion pancakes are made by making dough with scallions, flattening it, rolling it, flattening it again, then frying it. The process is like other flatbreads, for instance Indian poli, which uses very similar rolling techniques. Dozens of these little bags were piled up by the side of the grill, and this is where all the hidden work is revealed. Time goes into making each one of the dough balls melting in the hot sun, time goes into bringing them here, and time goes into procuring the ingredients for each one. All for us to enjoy a delicious and quick meal.

![Wonderfull scallion pankake](_images/IMG_4308.jpeg)

The chef working the grill was focused and efficient. Her hands were always moving, and she was aware at every second. Aware of the order of orders, what was on the grill, and what she needed to do next. She would take out a ball, flatten it, and let it fry on one side for some time. On the other side of the grill, she had a massive omelet being made – I did not see her crack any eggs, so I assume they were cracked and mixed before. And after frying one side of the pancake she would toss it around with her spatulas before dipping it into the egg and flip it over, adding additional fillings while the uncooked side fried up. Then the pancake would be folded and served. In this way I moved from tenth in line to holding my egg and basil pancake in 5 minutes. Lord it was delicious. These pancakes were thicker than the one I had the previous night. There was more egg too. The basil added a sharp flavor that was fresh and green to the otherwise fatty meal. The pancake was fluffy and chewy – less chewy than a good bagel, a lot less chewy -- on the inside, and crunchy on the outside – less crunchy than a good bagel too. It was not oily. And the flavor filled my mouth, with the sharp spots of green from the basil. Bread, egg, and basil are a classic combo, and one I have had many times, but without inspiration.

I ate this meal on the way to Daan forest park. There I sat and watched some birds for a while before returning to my hostel to nap. After I woke, the hostel manager took me and a couple French travelers to this Japanese Taiwanese restaurant nearby. We got our food cafeteria style from a stand in the street and brought it inside to eat. I had a baked fish, steamed egg, and tofu inari. The inari was delicious and I had some bamboo shoots that were also very good. The fish was not so good. I may have been eating it wrong, but every bite took a long time to extricate from the many bones in the fish. And it was only towards the end of the fish that I realized the best part was the skin, which I was tearing off. The fishbone was though; they are such delicate structures. The egg pudding was hard to eat, maybe it was the texture or the crab, either way, it was not my favorite. I wished I had just got some nigiri, or eel skewers which looked delicious. Still, I am curious about all the food that is possible to have, so I am happy that I got to have it. This was a memorable meal because likely, I would not have found it on my own, and because I ate things that I had never had before.

                  One thing about my overall framing of my writing here. First, I am a passable writer, but I enjoy writing a lot. But it is not something that I practice much as an engineer. So, I hope that I can look back on my posts and see growth. Don’t expect too much from me from the bat. Second, I want to attach my travels to more abstract conversations guided by Hannah Arendt’s *The Human Condition*. I have read Arendt each time I have traveled recently, and each has been relevant to my trip. It started with Arendt’s *The Origins of Totalitarianism* in Germany and continued with *On Revolution* in Boston. The geographic location of each was significant to each book, which made a particularly interesting reading for each. Arendt typically neglects the non-western world, so I doubt that *The Human Condition* will have geographic ties to Taiwan, but I think the subject matter, as her other works, is relevant to my life now. *The Human Condition* has three major sections dealing with labor, work, and action. Arendt argues about what the human condition is in a modern age and I want to add to that conversation. From a general stance as a young professional in a world of Space X and AI. And through food. The book deals with questions of space and automation, how each they our limits and nature. In terms of its three sections, I believe that food is one of the greatest ways that combines all parts of the human condition. Labor, as food is necessary to survival, work, as we put effort into making good food and maintaining historical cuisines, and action, as it brings us together to start something new. These are things I will try to focus on throughout my time here, and things that I hope will evolve as I come to understand what life in Taiwan is like (while comparing it to America) and read through the book. There is a lot to learn about, and I will do my best to understand it and record it here. Even through this first entry, I hope it is apparent how important food is to me, not just to survive but to experience history, and people. Viewing the world through food, changes how you see the world, because I believe it can reveal all aspects of the human condition.
