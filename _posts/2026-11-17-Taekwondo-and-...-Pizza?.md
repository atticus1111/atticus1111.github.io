Taekwondo has been very important to me through college. It is one of the few things I love about Boulder. Earlier this year we went to the national collegiate competitions. The previous year, I had lost miserably twice over and this year, I wanted to win. Actually, I didn’t want to win; I was going to win. Before stepping into the ring, I felt a rising wave of anxiety. But it crested and dissipated in the walk-up. I shook hands with my opponent. The match began. He was small, I went for the head. I am tall, he ducked in and punched me. It wasn’t enough, I won the first round. ‘Go for his head. Stay calm. You’re doing great.’ Then the second round. I got a good hit to the face. But they took away the points. I lost. ‘Don’t get shaken. You should have won that.’ And the third round started. I went to the head and held my ground. I won and felt like Ali. I won and the best part of winning was not the medal but my team cheering me on, their involvement in my fight. I won and I worked hard to win. Two years prior I was an almost incompetent athlete. I climbed this mountain, and the best part of the summit was a feeling of belonging. It is wonderful to feel wanted by my team and for them to cheer me on. And I chase that feeling of belonging with and fighting with a team.

I intend to win gold in a higher category next year, and I don’t want my skill to atrophy too much. So, I joined the Taekwondo team here. More importantly, I hoped this team would be as much of a home as back in Boulder. This team mostly focuses on Poomsae – the forms punching-the-air part of Taekwondo – and I am a sparring athlete. I have been learning the first Poomsae form, which has been fun. Poomsae is more natural to me, it is much more similar to practicing music than sparring. Or the kinda music I typically play at any rate. In the first form I feel that I move in a pattern like this 王. I block down to my left, then punch with the right hand. I turn with my fists closed over my chest and block with the right, and punch with the left. I step forward – a longer step – with my left and punch with the right – don’t move the feet. Then I turn again with my arm out and swing my left to hit in front of my face. I punch with right, then turn and repeat. I step forward one last time, punch down, punch right, don’t move. Then the fun part, it’s like drawing out a tree lined path, I block up, I kick with right, then punch. I turn and repeat. Last, I make to punches and return to my starting position.

But, like Boulder, the best part of being in Taekwondo is the team. Despite sometimes significant language barriers, I have gotten close to this team. Recently, they taught me Mahjong. After someone explained the rules of the game to me, I jumped in after the second hour of play. I am very proud to say that I won three times in a row. Once again, it is not the winning which was the best part, it was the feeling of in-placeness that came with high fives and smiles. Arendt talks about atomized masses of lonely people who are isolated and placeless. In some of the most powerful three pages I have ever read, she ties this placelessness to loneliness and makes it the root of Totalitarian government. I think if more people had the opportunity to play Mahjong with the same people the kicked, people would be less lonely.
